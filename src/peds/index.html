    Pediatric ICU delirium — risk factors

    PICU delirium is common (point prevalence ~25%; higher with mechanical ventilation and developmental delay) and is mostly hypoactive or mixed — easily missed. This is a review aid summarizing reported associations, not a validated predictive score. Minimizing the modifiable factors — benzodiazepines above all — is the actionable lever. Factors your child profile already implies are flagged automatically; check any others that apply.

    Modifiable levers: prefer dexmedetomidine over benzodiazepines; deprescribe anticholinergics; target light, goal-directed sedation; mobilize early; minimize restraints; protect sleep and maximize family presence.

        Treatment — a stepwise pathway

        Delirium treatment is mostly non-pharmacologic. Drugs treat symptoms (short-term, refractory agitation that threatens safety), never the delirium itself, and no agent is FDA-approved for pediatric delirium. Weight-based starting doses are in Medications.

        Step 1 — Treat the cause & apply the bundle (first-line)

        Find and fix precipitants. Optimize analgesia; switch benzodiazepine sedation to dexmedetomidine; deprescribe anticholinergics; restore sleep and day–night rhythm; mobilize early; maximize family presence; and assess for iatrogenic withdrawal (WAT-1). This is the treatment — drugs are adjunctive.

        Step 2 — If agitation threatens safety and non-pharm has failed

        Consider a short course of an antipsychotic at the lowest effective dose, reassessed daily. PANDEM names haloperidol or an atypical without preferring either; in reported PICU practice the atypicals (risperidone, quetiapine, olanzapine) are usually chosen first — a prescribing-pattern and safety-profile preference, not a guideline ranking. See Medications for weight-based starting doses.

        Off-label, limited evidence. No pediatric randomized trial supports antipsychotics for PICU delirium; the data are retrospective and observational, and a large database study linked antipsychotic use to higher mortality (confounded by indication). Use the lowest dose for the shortest time.
        Step 3 — Reserve IV haloperidol

        Reserving IV haloperidol for when an enteral agent isn't feasible is an expert-practice choice — PANDEM ranks no agent, and for small children who are NPO, IV haloperidol is among the options its reviewers name. It carries higher QTc / torsades and EPS / acute-dystonia risk than the atypicals — continuous ECG, correct K / Mg, and have IV diphenhydramine or benztropine ready.

        Before & during any antipsychotic — monitoring

        Baseline 12-lead ECG (QTc), electrolytes (K, Mg, Ca), and review of concurrent QT-prolonging drugs (ondansetron, methadone, macrolides, azoles, certain antiarrhythmics). Read the QTc against age-, sex-, rhythm- and correction-formula-appropriate limits: the normal upper limit is < 450 ms in children 11 days to 16 years (after puberty adult limits diverge by sex; Bazett is unreliable in tachycardia and with conduction abnormalities). Treat a borderline or prolonged result as a prompt to repeat / verify the ECG, correct electrolytes, and review interacting drugs; QTc ≥ 500 ms, or a clear rise from this patient's own baseline, warrants urgent prescriber / pharmacy review and management under the local cardiology or medication-safety protocol, which sets the stop / reduce thresholds. Watch for acute dystonia / EPS (most with haloperidol — treat with IV diphenhydramine or benztropine), akathisia, and NMS (fever, rigidity, autonomic instability, ↑ CK). Over longer courses, watch weight gain and metabolic change (highest with olanzapine and quetiapine).

          Sedation & sleep
          Agent Pediatric dose Notes
          Dexmedetomidine preferred 0.2–1 mcg/kg/hr IV infusion, titrated to a light goal; a loading bolus is often omitted in the PICU to limit hemodynamic effects. Delirium-sparing — preferred over benzodiazepines. Bradycardia, hypotension (biphasic BP); caution in cardiac disease / AV block. Rebound agitation/hypertension if stopped abruptly after prolonged use — taper. Not a substitute for analgesia (opioid-sparing at most) or reliable amnesia. PICU continuous sedation is off-label; the only FDA-labeled pediatric use is procedural sedation of non-intubated children ≥ 1 month.
          Benzodiazepines
          (midazolam, lorazepam)          		 Limit / avoid as continuous sedation. Independent, dose-related delirium risk. Do not escalate to treat delirium-related agitation. Reserve for specific indications (status epilepticus, sedative / alcohol withdrawal, procedural need).
          Melatonin 0.5–3 mg PO at bedtime (younger); up to 3–5 mg (older children / adolescents) — general pediatric sleep dosing. Sleep / circadian support — not a delirium treatment. Limited pediatric ICU evidence (delirium-prevention data are adult-ICU only). Confirm product (immediate- vs extended-release).
          Antipsychotics — off-label, limited evidence (short-term, refractory agitation only)
          Agent Pediatric dose (enteral unless noted) Notes
          Risperidone Start low and titrate, by age / weight band. ≤ 2 yr ~0.01–0.04 mg/kg/day (mean ~0.02) — studied only in a 17-patient cohort; verify every dose with a pediatric pharmacist. < 5 yr ~0.1 mg q12–24h; ≥ 5 yr ~0.2 mg q12–24h. No single daily maximum spans infants to adolescents — set the ceiling from a named institutional protocol or formulary for the child's age and weight. Often first-line oral. QTc (least among atypicals — still monitor), EPS / dystonia, hyperprolactinemia, sedation, metabolic. Baseline + follow-up ECG.
          Quetiapine ~0.43–0.7 mg/kg/dose q8h (initiation ≈ 1.5 mg/kg/day divided q8h; reported median 1.3 mg/kg/day, IQR 0.4–2.3). Sedating (useful at night); often chosen in the cardiac ICU. QTc prolongation reported; orthostasis, metabolic. Baseline + follow-up ECG.
          Olanzapine Age-banded start: infants ~0.625 mg; toddlers ~1.25 mg; older / severely agitated ~2.5–5 mg, once–twice daily. ODT useful if swallowing is difficult. Prominent sedation and metabolic / weight gain; anticholinergic effects; QTc.
          Haloperidol reserve Reserve for severe agitation when an enteral antipsychotic isn't feasible. IV is off-label; use it only under a current institutional pediatric agitation / delirium protocol. That protocol must specify age- and weight-based eligibility, route, the repeat interval, a maximum cumulative loading dose and a maximum daily dose, ECG / electrolyte monitoring, contraindications, and the treatment of acute dystonia. No generic loading or maintenance dose is shown here — the published pediatric data are a few small case series whose loading totals and intervals differ several-fold, so a numeric range without those protocol bounds would be unsafe. Oral liquid available. High caution. Dose-dependent QTc prolongation / torsades — continuous ECG, correct K / Mg; IV use carries an FDA torsades warning. More EPS / acute dystonia than atypicals in children (have IV diphenhydramine / benztropine ready). NMS; lowers seizure threshold. Avoid in long-QT, significant hepatic impairment, Parkinsonism.
          Deliriogenic medications — review & minimize

          Benzodiazepines (strongest modifiable risk) · centrally penetrating anticholinergics (especially diphenhydramine, atropine, scopolamine) — glycopyrrolate is a quaternary amine with limited CNS penetration, so weigh its peripheral effects and the overall medication burden rather than treating it as equivalent · high or escalating opioids (analgesia first, but cumulative dose is a risk) · corticosteroids (dose-related neuropsychiatric effects) · other CNS-acting / GABAergic agents.
